Your Watch shows one step count. Health on your iPhone shows another. Export the records, add the values in a spreadsheet, and you may get a third number—sometimes a much larger one.

That disagreement does not automatically mean your steps are duplicated or your export is broken. These views may be answering different questions: what one device recorded, what Health calculated across devices, or what every individual record adds up to.

**Start by comparing one completed day, using the same time zone, and identifying how each total was calculated.** Today's moving total makes a poor debugging target.

## Start with the shape of the mismatch

| What you see | First thing to inspect |
| --- | --- |
| Only the raw CSV sum is much larger | Overlapping records from multiple sources |
| One day is high and the next is low | Time zone and day boundaries |
| Only today's totals disagree | Refresh time and incoming records |
| A completed day differs across apps | Each app's source selection and calculation |

These are investigation paths, not diagnoses. Choose one completed day and keep its date, time zone, and sources beside every number you compare.

## 1. Find out which devices contributed

In Health, open **Browse → Activity → Steps**, then find **Data Sources & Access**. Inspect the list before changing anything. Apple lets you order contributing sources; the highest source takes priority when multiple sources contribute that type. A newly added source can change the order. [Apple's source-management guide](https://support.apple.com/en-us/108779)

An iPhone in your pocket and a Watch on your wrist can both observe the same walk. Their records can be individually valid while overlapping in what they describe. Two sources do not imply twice the movement.

Here is a deliberately simplified example, not a reconstruction of Apple's algorithm:

| Recording interval | Watch record | iPhone record |
|---|---:|---:|
| 9:00–9:10 | 900 steps | 850 steps |
| 9:10–9:20 | 1,000 steps | 950 steps |
| Raw total | 1,900 steps | 1,800 steps |

Adding both columns yields 3,700. But both devices observed the same twenty minutes. Neither that sum nor an average of the columns is automatically the correct combined result.

This is why a raw export and a daily summary are different products. Raw records preserve observations and their provenance. A daily summary applies rules to those observations. The same source check helps investigate an [unexpected Flights Climbed count](/blog/apple-watch-flights-climbed-wrong/) before blaming the Watch sensor.

## 2. Separate overlapping observations from repeated uploads

People use “duplicate” for two different problems:

- **The same HealthKit object appears twice.** A retry or repeated file import may have copied an existing record. Inspect the object's identity, not just its value.
- **Different objects describe overlapping activity.** The Watch and phone can create distinct records for the same walk. Removing repeated identifiers will not resolve that overlap.

PulsHealth handles repeated uploads separately from source overlap: re-sending the same raw sample does not create a second copy. Two devices observing the same walk still create distinct samples. The [database reference](/docs/database/) explains that storage rule.

Conversely, two records containing “120 steps” are not necessarily duplicates. They could describe separate walks. Deleting rows simply because their values match destroys information.

Before editing your history, inspect the record's source, interval, and identifier where your export exposes one. There is also a different way to double-count activity: adding a workout to a daily total that already includes it. The [active versus total calories walkthrough](/blog/apple-watch-active-vs-total-calories/) shows that accounting problem.

## 3. Check whether you exported records or statistics

HealthKit offers statistics queries that merge sources before calculating values. It also lets developers request statistics separately by source. Consequently, an app asking HealthKit for a daily cumulative sum is doing different work from a script adding every raw step record. [Apple's statistics documentation](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/hkstatistics)

For an everyday spreadsheet, use a **daily, all-device HealthKit sum** if you want the phone's calculated daily series. Keep the raw records for questions about individual devices or intervals.

PulsHealth's server can also provide a daily value through `metric_daily`. It prefers that canonical HealthKit aggregate. When the aggregate is unavailable, its cumulative fallback uses the highest single-source daily sum. That avoids blindly adding overlapping devices, but can miss complementary activity, such as a phone recording the morning and a Watch recording the evening. Check the returned `source`: `aggregate` means a phone aggregate; `rollup` means the fallback. The [daily metric rules](/docs/database/) describe eligibility and boundaries.

An aggregate is not guaranteed to match a screenshot of Health taken at another time. Permissions, query boundaries, and newly arrived records still matter. When comparing, record both the calculation method and the time you checked it.

## What happened in my own export

I compared thirty complete local days of my own synced data, September 1–30, 2026, using `America/Los_Angeles` calendar boundaries. Adding all raw step records by their start day produced **413,146 steps**. The canonical daily HealthKit aggregates totalled **230,642 steps**, rounded to a whole step. The naive raw total was **79.1% higher**.

All thirty daily values had `source = aggregate`; this comparison did not use PulsHealth's fallback. On September 1 alone, the raw sum was 19,621 and the daily aggregate was 10,389.

The finding is specific: adding the raw records did not reproduce the stored daily statistics. Overlapping sources are a reason to expect differences, but this comparison does not isolate their exact contribution. Assigning a whole raw interval to its start day can also differ from interval-aware aggregation. I did not compare these values with the Health app on the phone, so this is not a claim that the teal series exactly matches its display.

## 4. Make sure “Tuesday” means the same interval

A timestamp is an instant; a calendar day depends on a time zone. A spreadsheet grouping UTC dates can assign an evening walk to the following day even though Health groups it with your local evening.

HealthKit statistics collections also depend on their interval anchor. A one-day interval anchored at 3 a.m. creates boundaries at 3 a.m., rather than midnight. “Daily” alone does not specify the boundary. [Apple's interval explanation](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/executing-statistics-collection-queries)

For a useful comparison:

1. Choose a completed day without a recent time-zone change.
2. Use local midnight to the next local midnight, rather than assuming every day contains exactly 24 hours.
3. Check the export's offset and the chart's time zone.
4. In PulsHealth, check the account reporting time zone. Phone aggregates with date context retain the recorded phone day; during travel that can differ from the server reporting day. See the [database calendar rules](/docs/database/).

Travel requires an explicit convention: the day at your destination, the day in your home zone, or the zone carried by each record. These are different analytical choices. Document yours instead of treating a shifted total as missing movement.

## 5. Compare freshness before changing source priority

A raw sample can reach the server before a recomputed daily bucket. The current day also changes as more activity arrives. An export saved yesterday is a snapshot, even when today's dashboard is current.

Open the syncing app with the phone unlocked, run a sync if it provides that action, and compare a completed day again. If Health contains recent records but the receiving app does not, work through the [sync troubleshooting guide](/blog/apple-health-not-syncing/).

Changing source priority is useful when you have deliberately chosen which device to trust. It is not a universal repair for an old export, a server fallback, or a UTC grouping error. Changing it will not rewrite a CSV already on your computer.

## Keep enough information to explain the number

Label each result as a **raw sum**, **single-source sum**, **HealthKit aggregate**, or **server fallback**, with its date boundary and refresh time. That small amount of bookkeeping makes a later discrepancy much easier to trace.

For a spreadsheet you intend to use every week, export a defined daily series and retain raw records for investigation. The [export guide](/blog/export-apple-health-to-csv-excel/) shows how to choose between them.

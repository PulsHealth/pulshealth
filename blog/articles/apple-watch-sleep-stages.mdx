A sleep chart can make a complicated night look exact. You see a narrow strip of Deep sleep, a larger block of Core, and several interruptions. It is tempting to treat those colored minutes as a report card.

A better first question is: **what did the watch estimate, and how complete is the recording?** That tells you how much weight to give the chart before you start explaining it.

*Updated October 25, 2026: added the distinction between Sleep Score and sleep stages, and clarified overnight date boundaries. The personal-data example remains the September snapshot analyzed on October 8.*

## What Core, Deep, and REM mean

Apple maps its labels to conventional sleep stages as follows:

| Apple Health label | Corresponding stage | How to read the label |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core | Non-REM stages N1 and N2 | Sleep, rather than a failed attempt to reach Deep |
| Deep | Non-REM stage N3 | An estimate of slow-wave sleep |
| REM | Rapid eye movement sleep | A separate sleep stage |
| Awake | Wakefulness | Time classified as awake within the recording |

The watch does not measure brain waves. Apple's [2023 sleep-staging paper](https://images.apple.com/co/healthcare/docs/site/Estimating_Sleep_Stages_from_Apple_Watch_Sept_2023.pdf) describes an accelerometer-based model that classifies 30-second windows using movement signals, including subtle motion related to breathing. Core combines two stages; Apple chose that name partly to avoid implying that “light” sleep is unimportant.

That distinction matters when you export the data. The result is a series of **classified time intervals**, not a direct recording of your brain passing through each stage. ## How accurate are Apple Watch sleep stages?

Detecting sleep and choosing the correct stage are different tests. A headline percentage about sleep detection does not establish the accuracy of each Deep or REM interval.

Apple's validation paper reports that its most common stage error was assigning true Deep sleep to Core. That gives a concrete reason to avoid interpreting a short Deep segment as a precise physiological deficit. The paper describes a specific validated algorithm; it does not establish that every future software version performs identically. [Apple's validation results](https://images.apple.com/co/healthcare/docs/site/Estimating_Sleep_Stages_from_Apple_Watch_Sept_2023.pdf)

An independent 2025 study compared six wearables, including Apple Watch Series 8, with polysomnography during a laboratory night. Across the devices, identifying sleep was more successful than identifying wakefulness, and agreement on multiple sleep states remained imperfect. Participants wore two to four devices, so the full study's 62 participants were not necessarily all in each device comparison. These results concern the tested hardware, software, and study population. [Schyvens and colleagues, *Sleep Advances*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40303381/)

Use the chart for comparisons you can inspect: “Was this recording much shorter?” or “Have my recorded sleep times become more irregular?”

## Read the whole night before the percentages

Open **Health → Browse → Sleep** and inspect the daily chart, then switch to a longer view. For missing recordings, check that Track Sleep with Apple Watch is enabled. Apple's current guide calls for at least 30% charge before bed and at least one hour of wear. Those are recording requirements, not evidence that an entire night was captured. [Apple's sleep guide](https://support.apple.com/en-us/108906)

Use this order when reviewing a night:

1. **Check coverage.** Does the recording begin and end where you would expect? A watch charged midway through the night gives a partial view.
2. **Check duration.** Read the minutes asleep before comparing the shares assigned to each stage.
3. **Check the denominator.** Is a percentage calculated from time asleep, the whole recording, or time in bed?
4. **Add your own context.** Record whether you remember being awake, removed the watch, or traveled. Keep observations separate from explanations.

For example, 60 minutes is 20% of five hours but only 12.5% of eight hours. A lower percentage does not necessarily mean fewer minutes. That arithmetic alone can make two seemingly contradictory charts agree.

## Sleep Score is a different summary

Apple's current Sleep Score allocates up to **50 points to duration, 30 to bedtime consistency, and 20 to interruptions**. It is not a percentage of Deep or REM sleep. Bedtime consistency also uses earlier nights, so its inputs extend beyond the stage chart for one night. [How Apple describes Sleep Score](https://support.apple.com/en-us/108906)

A high score alongside a short-looking Deep segment is therefore not an arithmetic contradiction. Open the score's breakdown to see which component changed; open the stage chart to see how intervals were classified. They answer different questions about the recording.

## Why an export can contain more sleep than the night had

HealthKit distinguishes **in bed**, **asleep without a specified stage**, and the individual sleep stages. In-bed intervals can overlap sleep intervals; several apps can also contribute records. Apple's [sleep category documentation](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/hkcategoryvaluesleepanalysis) defines the categories used by those records.

Imagine an illustrative export with one eight-hour in-bed interval and seven hours of stage intervals inside it. Summing every row yields 15 hours. Nothing about the night was 15 hours long: the calculation added two descriptions of overlapping time.

An unspecified asleep interval needs similar care. It does not mean Core. Reclassifying every unspecified row as Core manufactures a stage that the source never supplied.

For analysis, I would first select one source, inspect overlapping intervals, and preserve the category labels. If two asleep intervals overlap, count their shared time once when calculating total sleep. If their stage labels conflict, flag the conflict rather than silently assigning a winner. Source reconciliation is also central to [understanding mismatched step counts](/blog/apple-health-step-counts-dont-match/), although sleep requires interval logic rather than a step-total calculation.

## What my own sleep export actually contained

In the October 8 analysis, I inspected my synced sleep records with a read-only query. It selected intervals starting from September 1, 2026 at midnight up to October 1 at midnight in America/Los_Angeles time. This describes records starting within the month, not a set of complete nights.

That returned **886 intervals**: 404 Core, 205 Awake, 160 REM, and 117 Deep. Those are record counts. A long interval and a short one each contribute one row, so the bars cannot tell you the proportion of the month spent in each stage.

## Build a useful view of several weeks

For a comparison across nights, keep these fields:

| Field | Why keep it |
| --- | --- |
| Sleep-session date | Keeps one overnight session together |
| Selected source | Makes device or app changes visible |
| Recorded asleep minutes | The union of the selected asleep intervals |
| Core, Deep, REM, unspecified minutes | Preserves what the source actually reported |
| Coverage or conflict note | Stops an incomplete record looking complete |
| Personal note | Separates your recollection from a device estimate |

Choose a rule for assigning sessions to dates. For example, a session from 11 p.m. Monday to 7 a.m. Tuesday can belong to Tuesday, its wake date. Grouping each stage by its own start date would split that session across Monday and Tuesday instead. For nightly analysis, retrieve the full session across the boundary first, then assign its date. Document how you handle naps and multiple sessions.

When summarizing a week, show how many nights contributed. “Average across four recorded nights” is more honest than presenting it as a complete seven-night average. Leave unrecorded nights missing. Entering zero would claim you did not sleep.

For a comparison with [HRV](/blog/apple-watch-hrv-complete-guide/), keep the observation counts for both series. Missing nights should remain visible in the comparison.

## What to do with a worrying chart

Save a few representative nights and note what you experienced.

A stage chart cannot settle whether you have a sleep disorder. Clinical sleep studies collect additional signals and are used to investigate sleep-related problems; a clinician determines which testing is appropriate. If sleep problems are persistent or affect daily life, bring the history and your observations to that conversation. [NHLBI's explanation of sleep studies](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep-studies)

For your next comparison, save the whole-night view and its recorded duration alongside the stage percentages. That gives you a way to tell a shorter recording from a different stage mix.

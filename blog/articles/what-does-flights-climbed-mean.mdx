**Flights Climbed is the number of stair flights recorded by your devices—not the number of individual stairs you stepped on.** If Apple Health shows five flights, it is reporting five units of that estimated activity. It is not saying you took five steps or visited five different floors.

In everyday language, a flight is a set of stairs between levels. The device records an estimate of climbing activity rather than identifying those architectural boundaries.

That is the short answer. The more interesting question arrives after a hike: why can a long climb and an ordinary day going upstairs produce numbers that don't seem to agree?

The temptation is to turn everything into floors: divide a hike's elevation gain by a familiar staircase height, compare the answer with Flights Climbed, and call the difference an error. But those two numbers describe different records. A better comparison starts with **when the flights were recorded, which device recorded them, and what else was happening**.

## What the number actually measures

Apple's HealthKit definition describes Flights Climbed as a cumulative count. Its supported automatic sources include iPhone, Apple Watch, and a Watch connected to a GymKit machine. A total viewed on your iPhone is therefore not necessarily a measurement made by that phone. [Apple's type definition](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/hkquantitytypeidentifier/flightsclimbed)

Apple Watch combines barometer and motion information to estimate ascent-related activity. It does not visually recognize a staircase or count its treads. Apple's sensor overview explains how pressure and motion contribute to estimates of elevation change, terrain grade and stair flights. [Apple's activity measurement paper, Barometer section](https://www.apple.com/health/pdf/Heart_Rate_Calorimetry_Activity_on_Apple_Watch_November_2024.pdf)

This makes Flights Climbed useful as a record of estimated climbing activity, but a poor substitute for a surveyed height. A physical floor, a stair flight, and a metre of workout elevation gain are not interchangeable labels.

| Number | What you can learn from it | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Steps | Recorded stepping activity | How much height you gained |
| Flights Climbed | Recorded stair-flight count | The staircase, building or exact ascent height |
| Workout elevation gain | Ascent reported for that workout | How many Flights Climbed records Health should contain |
| A route trace | Where a recorded workout went | Where every flight recorded that day happened |

## An indoor stair day and a hiking day can tell different stories

Imagine two days with the same recorded total: twelve flights. On the first, you remember several trips between floors. On the second, you logged a hike. The total alone cannot distinguish them.

For the stair day, repeated small entries near the times you remember using stairs would be consistent with your account. They would not prove every trip was recorded. For the hiking day, inspect whether the flights cluster within the workout. Keep flights before and after the hike separate: parking garages, home stairs, and other activity belong to the same calendar day too.

**Do not label every non-workout flight “indoors.”** An outdoor walk may have no recorded workout. Conversely, a workout can be indoors. A missing route does not establish an indoor setting either. If you cannot identify the context, keep an “unknown” category.

A defensible comparison is “flights overlapping a recorded hike versus flights outside that interval.” Calling it “hiking versus indoor stairs” requires additional evidence, such as your own notes.

## Try a comparison you can actually explain

Pick two completed days: one with a recorded hike and one when you remember using indoor stairs. Avoid using today's changing total. On the iPhone, search Health for **Flights Climbed**, open **Show All Data**, and inspect the entries for each day. Use **Data Sources & Access** to identify contributing devices. [Apple's Health data guide](https://support.apple.com/en-us/108779)

Keep a small comparison sheet:

| Field | Stair day | Hiking day |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Date and time zone | One completed local day | One completed local day |
| Context evidence | Notes about actual stair trips | Workout type, start and end |
| Health daily total | Copy the displayed value and time checked | Copy the displayed value and time checked |
| Recording sources | Note phone, Watch and other contributors | Note phone, Watch and other contributors |
| Timing | Entries near remembered trips | Entries within, outside or crossing the workout boundary |
| Separate ascent measure | Usually unavailable | Workout elevation gain, if recorded |

Leave missing values blank and mark them unavailable. Zero means a measured or calculated zero; it should not stand in for a record you couldn't find.

For entries crossing the workout's start or end, keep a separate boundary group. A record containing three flights over an interval does not tell you which second each flight occurred. Assigning all three to the hike—or dividing them evenly across time—adds an assumption. You can describe that assumption, but you cannot recover the missing timing by arithmetic.

If you repeat the comparison, use the same device setup and method. Several observations are more informative than one unusually good or bad day. This is a personal data investigation, not a device accuracy study: memories, workout records and sensors each have limitations.

## Why a spreadsheet can invent extra flights

Suppose a Watch and phone each record two flights during the same trip upstairs. Adding the two records produces four. That does not show that you climbed four flights, or that either device measured four.

For an overall daily comparison, use a clearly defined combined series, such as an all-source HealthKit cumulative statistic. For investigating a device, keep its records separate. Don't add source-specific totals back together and assume you have reconstructed Health's combined result: HealthKit merges sources when computing its default statistics. [Apple's statistics documentation](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/hkstatistics)

The same distinction matters for steps. Our [step-count investigation](/blog/apple-health-step-counts-dont-match/) includes a month of recorded data where the naive raw sum was much larger than the synced daily HealthKit aggregates. It also explains why simply choosing the larger device total can miss activity.

## What to do when the result still looks wrong

Start with the specific interval and source. If only your export looks inflated, check source overlap and calendar boundaries. If the Watch itself wrote a surprising entry, the recording conditions are the next question. The [unexpected stair-count guide](/blog/apple-watch-flights-climbed-wrong/) covers that investigation, including Apple's documented warning about water affecting the barometric altimeter.

Avoid deleting a device's entire history to fix one puzzling day. First preserve enough information to explain the discrepancy: the source, amount, interval, workout context and calculation method.

The rewarding part of this comparison is the story hidden by the daily total. Twelve flights spread across ordinary errands and twelve flights around a hike may represent very different days. The records can help you explore that difference—as long as you let “unknown” remain a valid answer.

To investigate your own history, [export selected records](/blog/export-apple-health-to-csv-excel/) and keep their source and timestamps.
